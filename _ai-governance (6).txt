If something remains, the analysis returns to the remedy stage.
If nothing legally remediable remains, the matter reaches remedial completion.

3. Attributable Injury. The claimant identifies injury legally attributable to the breach. This introduces causation and prevents the theory from treating every later disadvantage as automatically traceable to an earlier wrong.
4. Continuing Consequence. The analysis determines whether legally cognizable consequences remain after judgment or other intervention.
5. Remedial Correspondence. The remedy must correspond to the injury. Property loss might require restitution or compensation; continuing unlawful conduct might require injunctive relief; institutional violations might require structural remedies where legally authorized.
6. Effective Implementation. Ordering a remedy and delivering it are different events. Completion therefore requires examining whether the lawful remedy was actually implemented.
7. Completion Determination. Finally:
Does a legally cognizable and legally remediable deficit attributable to the established violation remain?
If yes, the matter has not reached remedial completion under the theory.
If no, Completion has occurred.
That produces:
Right → Breach → Injury → Continuity → Remedy → Implementation → Completion

The constitutional proposition
The deepest version of Completion Theory could begin with a distinction:
A right declared is not necessarily a right realized.
A legal system may recognize a right, adjudicate its violation, and issue a remedy. Completion Theory asks the additional question:
Did the lawful remedy become sufficiently effective to resolve the legally cognizable injury for which it was provided?
That creates three separate concepts:
Declared Justice — the law recognizes the right.
Adjudicated Justice — an authorized institution determines that the right was violated and establishes the legal consequence.
Completed Justice — the resulting remedy is lawfully implemented and satisfies the legally defined remedial obligation.
So:
Declaration ≠ Adjudication ≠ Completion
Each performs a different function.
The theory would need a principle of non-repetition

Has justice been completed?
Completion Theory could say not necessarily, because remedying yesterday’s injury while permitting the same legally established violation to continue may leave part of the remedial obligation unresolved.
That gives us another proposed principle:
The Non-Recurrence Principle
Where cessation or prevention is within the legally authorized remedy, completion requires not only remediation of established injury but implementation of the measures legally required to prevent continuation or recurrence of the violation.
Notice the limiting language: where legally authorized.

That becomes fundamental:

Completion cannot be achieved through an otherwise unlawful remedy.

Absolutely. Here is an A–Z architecture for developing Completion Theory of Justice into a serious jurisprudential framework. I’m treating “founder” as something the scholarly record ultimately substantiates, rather than assuming priority before the literature review is completed.
Completion Theory of Justice — A to Z
A — Accountability. Establish legal responsibility for a proven violation. Accountability is necessary, but under the theory it is not synonymous with completed justice.
B — Burden of Proof. Every claim must satisfy the evidentiary standard applicable to the proceeding. Completion Theory does not eliminate evidence requirements.
C — Completion. The central concept. Completion occurs when the legally cognizable and remediable consequences attributable to an established violation have been addressed to the extent required and permitted by governing law.
D — Deficit. Identify the Completion Deficit: what remains between the legally required remedy and what has actually been accomplished.
E — Evidence. Establish the factual record supporting the right, breach, injury, causation, continuing consequences, remedy and implementation.
F — Finality. Determine when further legal proceedings should end. Completion Theory must reconcile remediation with res judicata, limitation periods, jurisdiction and other doctrines protecting legal finality.
G — Governing Law. Identify the constitution, statutes, regulations, treaties, common law, precedent and procedural rules controlling the particular matter.
H — Harm. Define the legally cognizable injury rather than treating every social, historical or economic disadvantage as automatically actionable.
I — Implementation. Determine whether the remedy ordered or agreed upon was actually performed. Remedy ordered ≠ remedy implemented.
J — Justice. The overarching equation:
Justice = Recognition + Accountability + Remedy + Implementation + Completion
This is conceptual rather than mathematical.
K — Knowledge. Establish what relevant actors knew, when they knew it, and whether knowledge matters to liability, notice, intent, foreseeability or remedial responsibility under governing law.
L — Lawful Authority. No remedy becomes valid merely because someone considers it just. The institution ordering or administering the remedy must possess legal authority to do so.
M — Measurement. Develop appropriate methods for determining what was lost, what was restored, what remains and whether nonmonetary obligations were fulfilled.
N — Non-Recurrence. Where governing law authorizes prospective relief, completion may include legally required measures preventing continuation or recurrence of the established violation.
O — Ordered Remedy. Precisely identify what the court, legislature, agency, settlement or other competent authority actually requires.
P — Proportionality. The remedy should bear a legally justified relationship to the established injury. Completion Theory rejects both under-remediation and over-remediation.
Q — Quantification. Quantify economic injury where appropriate while recognizing that constitutional, dignitary, institutional and other injuries cannot always be reduced to money.
R — Restoration. Restore what law permits and requires—potentially through restitution, compensation, rehabilitation, injunctions, institutional reform or another recognized remedy.
S — Standing. Determine who possesses the legal right to assert the claim. Historical relationship or membership in a population does not by itself establish standing.
T — Traceability. Establish the causal chain:
Violation → Injury → Continuing Consequence → Present Claimant → Remedy
For intergenerational claims, every significant link requires evidentiary and legal support.
U — Universality. Completion Theory should work independently of any particular organization, government, population or political objective. A genuine jurisprudential theory must be capable of protecting opposing parties under the same principles.
V — Verification. Independently determine whether the authorized remedy was actually implemented. This is where Completion Theory goes beyond merely identifying what a judgment said should happen.
W — Wrong. Identify the underlying legally cognizable violation. Without a legally recognized wrong or remedial obligation, Completion Theory cannot manufacture one.
X — Examination. Apply the Completion Test after adjudication or recognition:
Right → Breach → Injury → Causation → Continuing Consequence → Remedy → Implementation → Completion
Y — Yield. Determine what the remedial process actually produced—not merely what was promised, appropriated, ordered or announced.
Z — Zero Legally Remediable Deficit. The conceptual endpoint:
Completion Deficit → 0
This does not mean history has been erased, every consequence has disappeared, or every person feels satisfied. It means no additional legally cognizable and legally remediable deficit has been established under the governing Completion analysis.

The governing principles
The full theory we’ve developed now contains several identifiable propositions:
The Completion Principle: Justice does not necessarily terminate at recognition or judgment; legally authorized remedies must be meaningfully implemented.
The Remedial Correspondence Principle: Remedy should correspond to legally established injury.
The Lawful Authority Principle: Completion cannot be accomplished through an unlawful or unauthorized remedy.
The Proportionality Principle: Neither insufficient nor excessive remediation constitutes proper completion.
The Implementation Principle: An ordered remedy and an implemented remedy are analytically distinct.
The Verification Principle: Completion requires determining what was actually performed.
The Non-Recurrence Principle: Where legally authorized and required, remediation can include preventing continuation or recurrence.
The Causation Principle: Continuing and intergenerational consequences require demonstrable legal and evidentiary connections to the established wrong.
The Finality Principle: Once the legally cognizable and legally remediable deficit has been resolved—or governing law precludes additional relief—the legal system must recognize finality.
And the framework protects both sides:
No claimant receives less than the law establishes is due merely because judgment was entered. No respondent owes more than can lawfully be attributed, established and remedied.
