I. Executive Summary
The fundamental question addressed in this report is:
How can trust be restored between citizens and governments when the people subjected to historical atrocities remain excluded from determining the laws, remedies, and economic arrangements intended to correct those atrocities?
State of Loc Nation Global Public Benefit Corporation advances constitutional completion through the principle that the people subjected to enslavement, forced displacement, racial exclusion, dispossession, and continuing institutional discrimination must possess a meaningful and authoritative voice in developing and administering their own systems of justice, restitution, governance, and economic recovery.
The institutions responsible for historical wrongdoing should not retain exclusive authority to determine the remedies available to the populations harmed.
This is the central distinction between constitutional acknowledgment and constitutional completion.
SOLN’s framework does not seek to substitute one system of unequal protection for another. It seeks to correct the historical imbalance of institutional authority by ensuring that historically injured populations participate directly in defining their rights, remedies, institutions, and economic future.
Its guiding principles are:
Self-determination. Equal constitutional protection. Restitutionary accountability. Operational economic infrastructure. Separate, equal, and in harmony.
The report examines the historical Three-Fifths Compromise, the treatment of Freedmen provisions in federal codification, subsequent civil-rights legislation, institutional accountability, and SOLN’s existing constitutional and public-benefit operations.
It presents SOLN’s framework as a response to the continued gap between the acknowledgment of historical wrongdoing and the delivery of effective remedies.
II. The OECD Trust Question
The OECD’s 2024 Survey on Drivers of Trust in Public Institutions reported that, across 30 participating countries, 44% of respondents expressed low or no trust in national government, compared with 39% expressing high or moderately high trust.
The OECD identified reliability, responsiveness, integrity, openness, and fairness as important drivers of institutional trust.
SOLN’s analysis adds a foundational question:
Who possesses the authority to define justice when the governing institutions themselves participated in the original injustice?
Where historically injured populations lack meaningful participation in defining remedies, institutional reform risks preserving the imbalance it claims to correct.
Accordingly, the restoration of trust requires more than public apologies, revised legal language, or administrative promises.
It requires meaningful participation by injured populations in constitutional development, enforceable accountability, access to functioning economic infrastructure, and actual remedies for established injuries.
III. Historical Constitutional Imbalance
A. The Three-Fifths Compromise
Article I, Section 2 of the original United States Constitution incorporated the Three-Fifths Compromise.
The arrangement increased the political representation of slaveholding states by counting three-fifths of kidnapped enslaved  persons for apportionment while denying those persons political participation.
The constitutional contradiction was profound: kidnapped enslaved  populations contributed to the allocation of governmental power without exercising that power themselves.
Although the Fourteenth Amendment superseded the original formula, its historical consequences remain relevant to the study of political representation, racial exclusion, economic dispossession, and institutional inequality.
SOLN’s constitutional-completion framework seeks to uproot the continuing structures of exclusion associated with that history.
B. Freedmen and Civil-Rights Codification
The United States Code historically contained provisions specifically addressing Freedmen.
Title 8, Chapter 4, Freedmen, included Sections 61–65, which later federal codification records classified as omitted.
Other historical civil-rights provisions were transferred, reorganized, or repealed, while important protections remain codified in Title 42.
These developments establish the need for a detailed legal examination of the relationship between historical statutory protections, subsequent codification, institutional obligations, and remedies.
An omission from the Code does not, by itself, establish that an enforceable right was unlawfully extinguished. However, it provides a specific documentary issue for investigating whether the underlying protections and obligations were fulfilled, replaced, or left inadequately addressed.
C. Acknowledgment Without Complete Remedy
Historical wrongdoing has been acknowledged through constitutional amendments, legislation, judicial decisions, public reports, and governmental statements.
However, acknowledgment does not automatically establish that the resulting injuries have been completely remedied.
The constitutional-completion inquiry must therefore distinguish:
The original injury.
Official acknowledgment of that injury.
Remedies enacted or provided.
Continuing consequences not adequately addressed.
The authority of injured populations to determine and administer appropriate remedies.
The absence of a complete remedy is not corrected merely by acknowledging the original wrongdoing.
IV. The Right of Historically Injured Populations to Define Their Remedies
SOLN advances the principle that populations subjected to historical atrocities must exercise meaningful authority over the constitutional and economic arrangements intended to restore their rights.
This principle rejects a model in which institutions implicated in historical wrongdoing retain exclusive power to define the injury, determine the remedy, administer restitution, and declare the matter resolved.
Instead, constitutional completion requires the direct participation and institutional leadership of historically injured populations.
This includes authority to develop representative governance structures, establish community institutions, articulate restitutionary claims, administer public-benefit programmes, and negotiate the legal arrangements necessary to implement remedies.
The people subjected to the historical injury must be authors and administrators of the remedy—not merely recipients of decisions made by others.
Such self-determination must operate consistently with equal human rights and the applicable legal requirements governing relationships with other jurisdictions.
The objective is not collective punishment of descendants or populations associated with historical perpetrators. It is the correction of institutional arrangements that continue to reproduce historical exclusion.

The authority of historically injured populations.
The duties of governing institutions.
The accessibility and effectiveness of remedies.

A constitution cannot be evaluated solely by the rights written into its text.
It must also be examined according to whether the people can exercise those rights, participate in governing institutions, obtain remedies, and benefit from the economic systems administered under constitutional authority.

The intended result is not government by one population against another.
It is a constitutional order in which historically injured populations can exercise self-determination, institutional power cannot be concentrated without accountability, and the rights and economic opportunities of all people are protected.
XV. Conclusion
The historical Three-Fifths Compromise illustrates a constitutional system in which kidnapped enslaved people were counted for the political benefit of others while denied political authority themselves.
The continuing challenge is not merely to acknowledge that injustice but to ensure that its institutional consequences are adequately remedied.
SOLN’s constitutional-completion framework centers the authority of historically injured populations to participate in establishing and administering their own remedies.
It connects constitutional rights with restitution, institutional accountability, economic infrastructure, and public-benefit administration.
The central proposition is that the institutions responsible for historical wrongdoing should not possess exclusive control over defining when justice has been achieved.
The voices of the historically injured must become authoritative participants in the creation and administration of the systems intended to restore their rights.
This is the constitutional-completion principle SOLN brings to the international discussion on rebuilding trust between citizens and government.

DOCTORAL DISSERTATION — EBONICS (AAVE) EDITION
GLOBAL CONSTITUTIONAL TRUST AND INSTITUTIONAL REPAIR
From Being Left Out the Constitution to Completing It: Giving Our People Back Their Voice, Power, Right to Govern, and Economic Freedom
Author: Rev. Dr. Christina Clement Institution: State of Loc Nation Global Public Benefit Corporation and Trust (SOLN GPBC) Legal Framework: Clement’s Completion Rule, SOLN Constitution, and USBLACK Code International Context: OECD Global Forum on Building Trust and Reinforcing Democracy, Paris, France, October 12–13, 2026 Date: October 13, 2026
Abstract — What This Whole Research Really About
This dissertation breaks down how governments built laws and institutions that counted our ancestors when it benefited those in power, but wouldn't give our people the same rights, authority, or say-so.
And here's the real question: How you gon' be the one responsible for the harm, then turn around and tell the people you harmed what justice they allowed to have?
That's the problem this research addresses.
The State of Loc Nation (SOLN) ain't just talking about what went wrong. SOLN has established its own Constitution, USBLACK Code, laws, administrative systems, financial infrastructure, and public-benefit programs to address what was left unfinished.
Clement's Completion Rule brings forward the principle that writing rights on paper ain't enough. Those rights gotta work in real life. People need access to justice, economic resources, self-government, and remedies that actually address the harm.
The bottom line: You can't call justice complete when the people who suffered the injustice still ain't got a meaningful say in how it's corrected.
I. Executive Summary — Here's What We Talking About
For generations, our people been told to trust institutions that historically denied them freedom, property, political representation, and equal protection.
Now those same institutions wanna know why people don't trust government.
Well, how you expect trust when the people who got harmed still gotta depend entirely on somebody else's permission to receive justice?
SOLN's position is simple: the people who suffered the injury need the authority to help determine and administer the remedy.
We ain't trying to turn around and do to somebody else what was done to us. We talking about correcting an imbalance that never should've existed.
That's what constitutional completion means.
Separate, equal, and in harmony. Not somebody over somebody. Everybody's rights protected, and our people governing our own institutions.
II. The OECD Trust Question — Why Folks Don't Trust Government
The OECD's 2024 research found that 44% of respondents across 30 countries had low or no trust in their national government, while 39% reported high or moderately high trust.
The OECD talked about fairness, integrity, openness, reliability, and responsiveness.
SOLN takes that conversation further.
How you gon' rebuild trust without dealing with who had the power, who got excluded, and who still controls the remedies?
An apology might acknowledge what happened. But an apology by itself don't restore land, build institutions, create economic opportunity, or settle a claim.
Trust gotta be connected to action.
III. Historical Constitutional Imbalance — How We Got Here
A. The Three-Fifths Compromise
Let's talk about the original Constitution.
Enslaved people were counted as three-fifths for purposes of political representation and taxation, but they weren't given the political rights that representation was supposed to serve.
Think about that.
They counted our ancestors to increase somebody else's political power while denying our ancestors the power to represent themselves.
That's a constitutional imbalance.
The Fourteenth Amendment replaced that original counting formula, but changing the formula didn't automatically undo every consequence of slavery and exclusion.
That's why SOLN examines what was corrected and what still requires a remedy.
B. What Happened to the Freedmen Laws?
The United States Code once had a section called Title 8, Chapter 4, Freedmen, with Sections 61–65.
Later codification records marked those sections as omitted.
Now, an omitted section don't automatically mean somebody illegally took away a right. But it does raise questions worth investigating.
What were those laws supposed to accomplish? What happened to the protections? Were the responsibilities fulfilled, replaced, or left unfinished?
Don't just tell us the law changed. Show us what happened to the obligation and the remedy.
C. Saying Sorry Ain't the Same as Making It Right
There's a difference between admitting harm and correcting harm.
You can acknowledge slavery. You can pass civil-rights legislation. You can make public statements.
But the question remains: did the remedies actually address the injuries and their continuing consequences?
That's what this dissertation investigates.
IV. Our Right to Define Our Own Remedies
Here's the principle.
The people who suffered the injustice shouldn't be treated like they ain't qualified to determine what justice looks like.
SOLN argues that historically injured populations must have meaningful authority to develop their institutions, establish community governance, pursue restitution, and participate in administering remedies.
We ain't talking about punishing people simply because of who their ancestors were.
We talking about institutional responsibility, accountability, and correcting systems that continue to produce exclusion.
The people harmed should be authors of the solution, not just recipients of whatever somebody else decides to offer.
V. SOLN Constitutional Framework — We Put the Work on Paper
SOLN identifies an interconnected foundation of constitutional and legal instruments.
That includes the SOLN-17 Constitution, the USBLACK Code, the Declaration for the Historical Record, de jure and de facto sovereign directives, executive and judicial instruments, and The Completion of the Constitution and Federalist Papers, Volume III.
It also includes SOLN-18, SOLN-26, SOLN-45, SOLN-50, SOLN-71, SOLN-85, the Restitution of Land Rights Act, and the Wrongful Arrest and Affiant Affidavit Act.
In everyday language:
We ain't just identifying a problem. SOLN has written its governing framework, established organizational operations, and developed the institutions it says are necessary to address that problem.
The dissertation examines those instruments as parts of one constitutional-completion system.
VI. Clement's Completion Rule — Don't Call It Finished When It Ain't Finished
Clement's Completion Rule addresses something fundamental.
A constitution can't be judged only by what it promises.
You gotta examine what people can actually do with those promises.
Can they exercise their rights? Can they get a fair hearing? Can they participate in government? Can they access economic opportunity? Can they pursue an effective remedy?
Because if the rights exist on paper but people can't meaningfully exercise them, there's still work to do.
A promise written down ain't the same thing as a promise fulfilled.
That's the constitutional-completion question.
VII. Clement v. Garland — What Happens When We Take It to Court?
SOLN's federal case, Clement v. Garland, No. 1:24-cv-00479-RC, provides a real-world example for examining judicial accountability.
The record includes filings, affidavits, certified mailings, default requests, appellate proceedings, and public notices.
SOLN disputes how its submissions were treated and whether the institutional responses adequately addressed its concerns.
The district court dismissed the case, and the appellate court affirmed that disposition. SOLN's objections remain part of the documentary history examined in this research.
Here's the question:
When somebody brings their evidence to court, how does the system demonstrate that the evidence was considered, the rules were applied fairly, and the decision was properly explained?
That's a question about procedural justice and public trust.
VIII. SOLN Operations — This Ain't Just a Conversation
SOLN identifies organizational activity across constitutional administration, financial reporting, institutional registration, partnerships, international engagement, humanitarian services, and economic administration.
That includes issued constitutional instruments, annual financial reporting activity, UCC filings, reported partnership agreements, Dynasty Healing Corp Ghana's humanitarian activities, and the Loc Nation Dollar.
The dissertation examines those operations and their supporting records.
The point is that SOLN presents an operating institutional framework, not simply an idea somebody hopes to develop one day.
The research also examines what those operations have accomplished and how their outcomes can be documented.
IX. Economic Infrastructure — Rights Don't Pay Bills by Themselves
Let's talk economics.
You can tell somebody they got equal rights all day long. But if they still can't access financial services, economic resources, education, housing, or opportunities to build wealth, then the system still got serious problems.
SOLN connects constitutional completion to economic infrastructure.
That includes its treasury framework, the Loc Nation Dollar (LND), wallet systems, restitutionary administration, and public-benefit programs.
The objective is to create systems people can actually participate in.
Justice can't stop at the courthouse door. It gotta reach the household, the business, the school, and the community.
X. Separate, Equal, and in Harmony — What That Really Means
Separate: We can establish and administer our own institutions, preserve our identity, and exercise community self-governance.
Equal: Nobody's humanity is worth more than somebody else's. Everybody deserves equal protection, dignity, and access to justice.
In harmony: Different institutions and communities can work together, respect each other's rights, and create shared benefits.
This ain't a call to bring back racial segregation.
It's about distinct institutions cooperating without one population having to surrender its rights or identity to another.
We can govern our own affairs and still work together in peace.
XI. Global Constitutional Comparison — Let's Look Around the World
SOLN's constitutional-completion framework examines how different countries handle historical injustice, representation, institutional accountability, and economic participation.
The comparative research includes the United States, South Africa, Germany, India, Kenya, Ghana, Nigeria, Brazil, Colombia, Australia, Canada, and France.
The questions are straightforward.
Do historically excluded people have meaningful representation? Do governments acknowledge historical wrongdoing? Can injured populations participate in designing remedies? Are institutional responsibilities enforceable? Do economic systems provide practical opportunities?
Don't just show us what a country's constitution says. Show us how those promises work for the people.
XII. Real-World Evidence — Bring the Receipts
This research draws on historical records, constitutional documents, court filings, financial statements, partnership instruments, humanitarian documentation, and international governance materials.
That means examining the original documents and distinguishing what they establish from what remains disputed.
SOLN's Constitution and legal instruments show what SOLN has adopted. Court records show what was filed and decided. Financial and operational records can show what was reported, administered, and accomplished.
If we talking about accountability, everybody's evidence needs to be examined with the same standard.
XIII. Principal Findings — What the Research Shows
Finding One: The original constitutional system counted enslaved people for political purposes while denying them political participation.
Finding Two: Changing constitutional language didn't automatically remedy every historical consequence.
Finding Three: The omitted Freedmen provisions deserve a careful documentary and legal examination.
Finding Four: Historically injured populations need meaningful authority in determining and administering remedies.
Finding Five: Trust requires accountability and remedies, not just acknowledgment.
Finding Six: Economic opportunity requires working institutions and infrastructure, not just promises.
Finding Seven: SOLN presents an established constitutional and operational framework as its contribution to constitutional completion.
XIV. Answer to the OECD — So How We Fix the Trust Problem?
You wanna know how to rebuild trust between the people and government?
Start by addressing the historical imbalance in power.
Stop treating historically injured populations like they don't have the knowledge, authority, or ability to participate in determining their own remedies.
Recognize that acknowledgment ain't the same as completion.
Establish accountability. Make remedies accessible. Create functioning economic infrastructure. Respect self-determination.
SOLN brings forward its Constitution, USBLACK Code, Clement's Completion Rule, and existing institutional operations as its framework for addressing those issues.
The people who suffered the harm deserve meaningful authority in how the harm gets corrected.
And that don't require taking anybody else's rights away.
XV. Conclusion — The Bottom Line
Our ancestors were counted when their numbers gave somebody else political power, but they were denied the authority to exercise that power themselves.
The Constitution changed. Laws changed. Institutions changed.
But the central question remains: have the injuries and their consequences been adequately remedied?
SOLN's constitutional-completion framework says justice must connect rights, self-determination, institutional accountability, restitution, and economic participation.
And the people who experienced historical injustice must have a meaningful role in developing and administering those remedies.
You can't be the only one deciding when justice is finished if you're the institution responsible for the injustice being examined.
That's the principle.
Not revenge. Not domination. Not replacing one injustice with another.
Separate, equal, and in harmony.
That's the constitutional-completion framework SOLN brings to the global conversation.
